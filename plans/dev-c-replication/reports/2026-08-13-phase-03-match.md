## 1. One-paragraph summary

The server runs a complete match on its own: it waits for players, counts down a warmup,
opens the round, resolves capture points from who is standing on them, bleeds the losing
side's tickets, ends on a winner, holds a scoreboard, tears the world down, and starts
waiting again — and it does all of that inside `dotnet test`, because the state machine,
the capture points, the id pool and the clean-state audit are engine-free and the Unity
`MatchController` only reads positions out of the scene and puts bytes on the transport.
Bandwidth at the full **16 players plus 32 bots** is **1.67 KB/s per client** against an
8 KB/s budget, so criterion 8 passes with four times the headroom, and the phase's own
task-5 instruction — *measure before optimizing, and if you are inside the budget spend
the time elsewhere* — is followed literally: of its seven proposed optimizations, two ship,
three are measured and rejected, and two are declined with the measurement that says why.
Join tickets are verified with HMAC, expiry, a this-server check and a
one-session-per-player reservation, all through the shared `JoinTicket.Verify`, so trap 4's
timing attack has no comparison to exploit. The master link is real but is reached through
a port, so a server with no master plays complete matches and is simply not advertised —
standalone is a composition choice rather than a null check. What is left is **criterion 1**
(a video of a full match), **criterion 5** (D confirming the server appears in the list)
and **criterion 10** (16 real clients for 20 minutes), none of which a test can produce.

## 3. Bandwidth — measured

`Phase03LoadTests.PrintTheSixteenPlayerBandwidthTableAndHoldTheBudget`. 16 clients,
48 actors, Dustbowl's real 1700 m box, 20 Hz, 30 s, the same movement mix phases 01 and 02
used, encoded through `ServerPayloadWriter` so payload framing is included.

| Configuration | KB/s/client | Actor slots sent | Saving vs previous |
|---|---|---|---|
| No interest management | 9.21 | 460,800 | — |
| + interest management | 1.78 | 40,553 | **80.6%** |
| + velocity culling, stale-corpse drop | **1.67** | 37,047 | 6.6% |

Against `plan.md` § 10's 8 KB/s, the shipped server uses **21%** of its downstream budget
at the full design load. Sixteen clients is 26.7 KB/s off the server, against the 109 KB/s
the plan budgeted.

This is the first phase to measure at 16 viewers rather than 8; phase 02's 1.92 KB/s was
the same world seen by half as many people. The two are consistent — the per-client figure
barely moves with viewer count, because interest management is per-viewer and each client
pays only for what it can see.

## 4. Task 5 — the optimization list, and what happened to it

The phase document proposes seven optimizations and then says: *"Measure before optimizing.
If you're already inside the budget, don't optimize — spend the time elsewhere."* At
1.67 KB/s against 8, that instruction is load-bearing rather than decorative. So each one
was measured first, and most did not survive it.

| # | Optimization | Estimated | Measured | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Drop velocity at Mid/Far | ~15% | **part of 6.6%** | **Shipped.** Expressible inside the frozen format — it clears a change-mask bit, so a v1 client decodes it without knowing |
| 2 | 12-bit height | ~5% | **~1%**, and costs 12.5 cm | **Rejected.** See below |
| 3 | Drop pitch beyond 50 m | ~4% | **not expressible** | **Rejected.** Yaw and pitch share one change-mask bit on the v1 wire; suppressing pitch alone needs a format change |
| 4 | Batch messages into one datagram | ~10% | not attempted | **Deferred.** It is a transport-layer change (Dev B's `PayloadFrame` already batches within a payload) and would buy header bytes we are not short of |
| 5 | Drop dead actors after 3 s | ~5% | **part of 6.6%** | **Shipped.** Corpses are never synchronised anyway (AD-4) — the client runs its own ragdoll — so every snapshot after the first few describes a pose nobody will move again |
| 6 | LOD ticking for AI | ~30% CPU | phase 02 | Already done |
| 7 | Cache interest results for 3 ticks | ~8% CPU | **declined** | **Declined with a reason.** Interest management is 85% of the netcode's 258 µs, so 8% of it is ~18 µs of a 33,333 µs tick. The cost is a stale interest level for up to 150 ms, which means a player sprinting from Far into Near keeps updating at 4 Hz for a fifth of a second. Trading visible responsiveness for 0.05% of a budget we are not near is a bad trade, and it stays available if a profiler ever says otherwise |

**The 12-bit height is the one worth writing up**, because the measurement contradicted the
proposal. It is normally justified on the grounds that 256 m across 4096 steps is the same
6.25 cm quantum the position field already uses, so the range is free. It is not: the value
has already been through `PackPos`, so the two quantizations compound and the measured
worst-case error is **12.5 cm**, exactly double the position quantum
(`TheTwelveBitHeightCostsResolutionAndSaysSoInMetres`). So it costs precision *and* caps
the world at 256 m tall, for about 1% — and it is a wire-format change on top. Rejected on
the numbers rather than on principle.

**The two that shipped are the two that are expressible inside the frozen v1 format.** That
is not a coincidence, it is the selection criterion: velocity culling and the corpse drop
change *which actors and which fields* go in a snapshot, never how a field is laid out, so
a client built against the frozen spec decodes either setting without being told. The
`ReplicationConfig` flags for the other techniques exist, and are honoured only by the
phase-04 experiment codec in the test project, which encodes *and* decodes both ends — so
each gets a real measured number and the server never emits a format nobody has agreed to.

## 6. Technical decisions

| # | Problem | Chosen | Rejected | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C-03-1 | The phase sketch makes `MatchController` a `MonoBehaviour` holding the whole lifecycle | Engine-free `MatchStateMachine`; the `MonoBehaviour` reads scene positions and sends bytes | Write it as the sketch does | The same split as C-01-6 and for the same reason. "Does the match end when a team runs out of tickets" and "are five rounds back to back clean" are the two things most likely to be wrong, and both become unanswerable the moment they live inside a component CI cannot construct |
| C-03-2 | `AssertCleanState()` as the sketch writes it is `Debug.Assert` | `ServerStateAudit.Capture()` returns a value; the caller decides | Keep the assert | `Debug.Assert` fires only in a development build and only where somebody is watching. The leak it looks for shows up on round three of a server that has been up for an hour with nobody attached. Returning a value means the load test asserts on it, the tick loop logs it, and neither has to be a development build |
| C-03-3 | Trap 2 — an id reused while stale packets are in flight | `ActorIdPool` with a 5 s quarantine, and a FIFO so ids rotate | Reuse immediately; or detect the stale packet | 5 s is past `FRAGMENT_TIMEOUT_MS` (2 s), which is the longest a fragment of an old snapshot can legitimately still be awaiting reassembly, and short of `TIMEOUT_MS` (10 s). Detection is not available: the stale packet is *valid*, it is just about the previous occupant |
| C-03-4 | A reset with a 5 s quarantine per id would starve the pool across 5 quick rounds | `ResetAll` skips the quarantine | Keep quarantining across a reset | The hazard is a stale packet naming an id that now belongs to somebody else. After a reset every client is being told to drop every actor it knows, so there is no "somebody else" to confuse it with |
| C-03-5 | Trap 3 — spamming capture-point messages | Send on a 2% move **or** an ownership flip, both tested on the quantized byte | Send on the 2% move only | Crossing the ownership threshold is a 1% move that flips a flag's colour and starts the enemy's tickets draining. A threshold test alone leaves clients on the wrong side of it for seconds. Measured: ≤ 11 messages per second per point while it is actively being taken, against 30 for the naive form |
| C-03-6 | Should `S_MATCH_STATE` carry a `winningTeam`? | **No.** It is derived from the two ticket counts | Send it | A stored copy of a computed value is a field that can disagree with its own source (conventions: no derived fields). With no field, a message claiming team 0 won while team 1 has more tickets is not expressible. Same call for `S_CAPTURE_POINT`'s owning team |
| C-03-7 | Ticket replay: the transport asks "is this ticket valid" before there is a connection | The check **reserves** the playerId then and there | Consult a list of connected players | Two simultaneous replays of one leaked ticket would both pass — neither has finished connecting when the other is checked. The reservation expires with the ticket that created it, so an abandoned handshake cannot leak a slot for longer than the ticket was valid |
| C-03-8 | The Unity project consumes prebuilt DLLs, so anything `Replication.dll` references must ship into the Editor | `IMatchReporter` port in Replication; the TCP client stays in Dev D's assembly; a third assembly bridges them | Reference `Ironfront.MasterClient` from `Replication` | Referencing it would drag a socket and `System.Text.Json` into the Editor for four calls made once every five seconds. The port also makes standalone mode a *composition* choice — `NullMatchReporter` — instead of a null check at every call site |
| C-03-9 | A live round when the last human leaves | Keep playing | Abandon the round | The bots are still fighting and the master is still owed a `GS_MATCH_ENDED`. A round that evaporates leaves the server advertising a state it is not in. Warmup *does* fall back, because a round started for one player is over before anyone can join it |
| C-03-10 | Heartbeat pacing: the sketch uses `InvokeRepeating(nameof(SendHeartbeat), 5f, 5f)` | `HeartbeatPacer`, an accumulator | `InvokeRepeating` | It keeps firing after the server stops, drifts against the tick clock, and cannot be asserted on. The accumulator *subtracts* the interval rather than zeroing — zeroing loses the overshoot on every beat and the heartbeat quietly runs slower than the interval it advertises |
| C-03-11 | `cpuPercent` on GS_HEARTBEAT, which the master sorts on | Send **−1** | Send a plausible number | Unity exposes no portable process-CPU counter. A fabricated value on a matchmaking input is worse than an absent one, because the master will act on it. Average tick time is real and is sent alongside. Flagged as **A12** |
| C-03-12 | Which optimizations to ship | Only the ones expressible in the frozen v1 format | Ship them all behind flags | A runtime flag that changes the wire format is a server able to emit something no client is required to understand. The rest are measured by a codec that owns both ends — see § 4 |

## 7. Things tried that FAILED

| Tried | Why it didn't work | Signs |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantizing capture ownership at 1% and sending on any change** | It *is* the right quantization, and it is not a send policy. At 0.2 ownership per second the byte changes 20 times a second, so the message rate was two-thirds of the tick rate — trap 3 almost exactly, arrived at from the other direction. I had written `MatchRules.CaptureSendThreshold = 0.02f` and then never read it | A test asserting "not every tick" failed at **20 sends per second**. The fix was to use the field I had already defined, plus the ownership-flip clause it needs to not be sloppy |
| **Measuring the tick breakdown as one timed pass per stage, then subtracting** | `BuildView` records each pair's send as a side effect, so calling it a second time in the same snapshot finds nothing due and returns an almost empty view. The encode stage was therefore measuring an empty snapshot | The table printed **0.0 µs** for delta encoding sixteen clients — a stage that is plainly not free. Rewritten as three accumulating stopwatches over one pass; it now reads 18.5 µs, and a `> 0` assertion guards the methodology |
| **Claiming the 12-bit height is free because 256/4096 is the position quantum** | The value has already been through `PackPos`, so the two quantizations compound | Worst-case error measured at **12.5 cm**, exactly double the 6.25 cm quantum. The claim was in a comment before it was in a test; the test is what corrected it |
| **Releasing a ticket claim positionally on disconnect** | The first draft popped the oldest admitted player on *any* disconnect, so a client leaving out of order released somebody else's claim. It would have let a leaked ticket in while locking a live player out | Caught while writing it, not by a test. Now the admission is paired with its connection id at connect time and the disconnect looks up by id, so clients may leave in any order. The remaining positional assumption is only at connect, where it holds — reported to Dev B as **B7** |
| **Ending the match from inside `ReportDeath`** | It would raise `MatchEnded` partway through a tick, with capture points already advanced and ticket bleed not yet applied — a scoreboard describing half a tick | The win condition is evaluated by `Tick`. Five tests had to gain an explicit tick, which is the honest shape of a state machine driven by a loop |
| **Asserting the ticket bleed after 3 seconds** | `Tickets0`/`Tickets1` round **up**, so at 0.5 tickets a second the displayed count does not move for two seconds. Asserting on the float would have tested the accumulator rather than the scoreboard | The test read "team 1 should be bleeding, has 50" while the accumulator was at 49.05. Extended to six seconds and left asserting on the integer, because the integer is what a player sees |
